               When Does Fourier Diagonalize the Physics?
                  Calibrated Meters for Fixed versus Learned Representations
                  in Operator Learning, and What the Learned Part Is Worth


                     Juan Carlos del Rı́o Romero · independent research
                                  ORCID: 0009-0009-2908-4168

                                 August 2026 — working draft


Abstract — A companion study showed that, for classification, a pooled dictionary of fixed
transforms usually beats a transform learned from scratch, and that a gated coupling of the
two self-calibrates: the gate closes where the dictionary suffices and opens where it does
not [10]. This paper re-instantiates that architecture for physical modelling, where the objective
inverts — predict the state rather than compress toward a label — and asks a question that
classification cannot answer well: what does the learned part actually say? We report six
results, all on controlled one-dimensional partial differential equations (PDEs). (i) The failure
of a fixed spectral basis to diagonalize the propagator is a clean, monotone heterogeneity
meter: for diffusion with a spatially varying coefficient a(x), Fourier’s off-diagonal fraction rises
0 → 0.273 with the heterogeneity knob while the true eigenbasis stays exactly diagonal. (ii) That
eigenbasis can be discovered without an oracle by minimising off-diagonality over a structured
Sturm–Liouville family — reaching 0.000000 at every heterogeneity and returning the physical
coefficient at correlation 1.0000 — and the same objective detects its own mis-specification.
(iii) The residual of a data-driven linear (dynamic mode decomposition, DMD) propagator is
a nonlinearity meter, and a gated learned branch fires with the same rectified, thresholded
law as the classification gate: shut until the nonlinearity knob ε ≈ 0.4, then rising linearly. (iv)
Making that branch deeper is not the lever; making it structurally correct is — held-out capture
of what DMD misses goes 11.4% → 71.6% when the branch’s features match the true quadratic
term, while stacked residual integrators reach only 15.6%. (v) Sparse regression then names that
term exactly, recovering the diffusion coefficient to three decimals and the nonlinear coefficient
on the identity line. (vi) Putting the named term into the model buys extrapolation: over a
30-step rollout never seen in training it is ∼ 4× more accurate than a black-box branch, and
∼ 3× on unseen amplitudes; rollout training then improves the structured model a further 3.4×
while making the black box worse. We are explicit about what is established elsewhere (operator
learning, Koopman/DMD, sparse identification, inverse coefficient problems) and about our own
negative results, discarded metrics and confound controls.


1    Introduction

Two families of front-end compete whenever a signal must be represented compactly. A fixed
transform — Fourier, wavelet, scattering — is cheap, interpretable and needs no data. A learned
transform is adaptable but under-determined and opaque. A companion study [10] compared
them for classification and found that a gated coupling behaves best, with the gate acting as a
diagnostic: it collapses to the dictionary when the dictionary suffices, and engages the learned
branch only where the discriminative structure is genuinely non-classical.
That study left a dissatisfaction. Under a classification objective, reading the learned transform
is ill-posed: many bases separate the same labels, so the learned basis drifts and its entries carry

What we claim, and what we do not. Several ingredients here are established and we
use them as tools, not contributions: neural operator learning and its spectral variants [1];
Koopman operator theory and dynamic mode decomposition [2, 3]; sparse identification of
governing equations [4, 5]; the recovery of a coefficient from spectral data, which is a mature
inverse problem [6]; and the general finding in scientific machine learning that inductive bias
beats raw capacity. Relatedly, it has recently been proved that an invariant network’s weights
recover the Fourier transform of the underlying group, and that the group can be read off those
weights [7]; we do not re-derive that. Our contribution is narrower and, we believe, useful: a set
of calibrated meters — quantities you compute on your own operator or data that answer
how much of a fixed representation is failing, of what kind, and whether the learned part is worth
reading — plus the controls that make those meters trustworthy.


Roadmap. Section 2 fixes notation and the diagonality metric. Sections 3 and 4 build and
then close the heterogeneity meter. Sections 5 and 6 build the nonlinearity meter and settle
depth-versus-structure. Section 7 reads the operator, Sections 8 and 9 measure what that reading
is worth. Section 11 collects the negative results.


2    Setup

Operators. We use two controlled families on a periodic grid of N points. The linear family is
diffusion with a spatially varying coefficient, ut = ∂x (a(x) ∂x u), discretized as L = −G⊤ diag(af ) G
with G the forward-difference operator and af the coefficient on cell faces; the one-step propagator
is P = exp(L ∆t). A knob β sets the heterogeneity, a = 1 + βf (x) with f a fixed smooth
normalized field; β = 0 gives constant coefficients, for which Fourier is exactly the eigenbasis.
The nonlinear family is ut = ν uxx − ε u ux , integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta and 2/3
dealiasing: ε = 0 is the linear heat equation, ε > 0 is Burgers.


The diagonality metric. For a basis B (rows orthonormal) and a propagator P , define

3     A heterogeneity meter

We sweep β and evaluate Equation (1) for the fixed real Fourier basis and for the true eigenbasis
Φ of L (an oracle, removed in Section 4).
At β = 0, Fourier diagonalizes P exactly (D = 0.000). As heterogeneity grows, DFourier rises
monotonically to 0.273 at β = 4, while DΦ ≡ 0 at every β; the alignment between Φ and Fourier
falls from 1.00 to 0.44 (Figure 1). The fixed basis is therefore optimal exactly where the medium
is homogeneous, and degrades in proportion to how far the physics has moved away from it. The
failure of the fixed basis is the measurement.


4     Discovering the basis without an oracle

The oracle is the weak point of Section 3. Learning a free unitary is the classification failure mode
— under-determined and drifting — so instead we search a structured family: Sturm–Liouville
operators L(θ) = −G⊤ diag(aθ )G with aθ a truncated Fourier parametrization of the coefficient
(6 or 10 parameters). The objective is only Equation (1) on the true propagator: no labels, no
trajectories, no oracle, started from Fourier (θ = 0).
The family is well motivated rather than arbitrary: the classical orthogonal bases (Fourier,
Hermite, Legendre, Chebyshev) are the eigenbases of Sturm–Liouville operators, so searching
over such bases is searching over second-order differential operators. Note also a degeneracy
that makes the operator, not the basis, the right thing to parametrize: at a fixed rank K, all
polynomial families of degree < K span the same subspace.


Result. Off-diagonality falls from 0.041–0.273 (Fourier) to 0.000000 at every β, and the
recovered coefficient matches the truth at correlation 1.0000 (Figure 2). Eigenvectors are
invariant under a → c a for c > 0, so a is identifiable only up to a positive scale and recovery is

The meter detects its own mis-specification. When the true a(x) contains harmonics
outside the search family, recovery degrades sharply: off-diagonality only falls 0.263 → 0.178
and the recovered profile correlates 0.13–0.41 with the truth. Widening the family to contain
the truth restores 0.000000 and 1.0000. So the failure mode is mis-specification, not the method
— and the objective value itself announces it, by plateauing well above zero. This is the precise
counterweight to Section 6: structure is the lever, but wrong structure is worse than useless.


5     A nonlinearity meter, and a gate that fires

We now move to ut = νuxx − εuux and learn a one-step map over a horizon long enough for the
nonlinearity to act. The linear reference is the closed-form best linear propagator A (DMD): it
can only represent a superposition of exponential modes, so its held-out residual is exactly what
a Laplace/Koopman-linear front-end cannot capture. On top we place a gated learned branch,
û1 = Au0 + g · NL(u0 ), with A frozen and only the branch trained on the residual under an L1
penalty on g.
Two things track ε monotonically (Figure 3). The DMD residual rises 0 → 0.153: a clean
nonlinearity meter. And the branch fires with a rectified, thresholded law — amplitude ≈ 0 up to
ε ≈ 0.4, then rising roughly linearly to 0.127. That is the same shape as the classification gate
law of the companion study, where the gate followed a controlled information gap with Pearson
r = 0.96 and a rectified fit. The branch does not fire until the physics is nonlinear enough to
need it, and then fires in proportion. When it fires it genuinely captures out-of-sample, removing
a steady 11.6–13.4% of the DMD residual.


6     Depth is not the lever; structure is

The branch above captures only ∼ 13% of what DMD misses. Since u ux is a low-order polynomial
nonlinearity that a two-layer network can express, the ceiling is one of generalization, not capacity
— confirmed by the fact that removing regularization makes the branch fire more while going
negative on held-out data. We therefore compare four branch designs on the identical frozen-DMD
residual (Table 2, Figure 4).

7    Reading the operator

We estimate ut numerically (a small forward difference, not the analytic right-hand side), build
a library of candidate spatial-differential terms [ u, ux , uxx , uxxx , u2 , uux , uuxx , (ux )2 ] via
spectral derivatives, and run sequential thresholded least squares.
The recovery is exact in the accessible regime (Figure 5): the diffusion coefficient is pinned
at 0.050 = ν, the coefficient of uux lies on the identity line −ε (measured −0.200, −0.400,
−0.699, −0.991 for ε = 0.2, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0), and every non-physical library term is driven to zero
by the sparsity. Honest limit: for ε ≥ 1.5 the nonlinear coefficient is under-estimated (−1.688
against −2.0) and ν drifts to 0.042. This is a resolution effect — strong Burgers shocks are
under-resolved at N = 64 and the numerical ut degrades where gradients steepen — not a failure
of identification.

never saw.


9    Hardening: rollout training multiplies structure

Finally we ask whether training on rollout (rather than one-step) changes the picture, and
whether the models respect the physics — viscous Burgers must dissipate energy, never create it.
Rollout training is applied as fine-tuning on top of the converged one-step model, unrolling 5
steps with gradient clipping; three seeds, mean ± standard deviation.
Rollout training is therefore not a substitute for structure; it is a multiplier on structure (Figure 7).
It also collapses the seed variance of the structured model (out-of-distribution standard deviation
0.075 → 0.007): the right operator trained on the right objective becomes seed-insensitive. The
one-step structured model reproduces Section 8 within seed variation (0.138 ± 0.012 here against
0.150 there), which is precisely why this table carries error bars. The physical check passes for
all models — spurious energy growth is 0.0% of steps everywhere except the black box under

10     Related work

Learning solution operators for PDEs is an active field; Fourier neural operators [1] are, in the
language of this paper, the fixed-spectral branch promoted to an operator. Koopman theory and
DMD [2, 3] are the learned-linear branch, and the reduced-order-modelling literature has long
used them for prediction. Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics [4] and PDE-FIND [5]
provide the reading step of Section 7; we use them unmodified. Recovering a coefficient field
from spectral or boundary data is the classical inverse-conductivity problem [6], a mature area;
our Section 4 is a small, well-specified parametric instance of it and claims no advance there. On
the learned-representation side, it has been proved that invariant networks recover the Fourier
transform of the underlying group and that the group is readable from the weights [7]; that
result is stronger than anything we show about why classical bases appear, and we position our
contribution as complementary and empirical. What we believe is not covered by the above
is the packaging: a small set of calibrated meters with a shared shape — fixed structure plus
measured failure — validated across two different objectives (a label-compression gate with

11     Limitations and negative results

We state these explicitly, in the order they cost us time.
An artefact we had to remove. The heterogeneity meter was first built from a prediction
error with Fourier-smooth initial conditions. It produced a negative gap — Fourier apparently
better than the eigenbasis — because those initial conditions let Fourier compress the data
irrespective of the dynamics, and a full linear head made the basis irrelevant. Moving to the
operator-level metric of Equation (1) removed the confound.
An unfair comparison we had to fix. The first gated experiment trained a coupled network
by stochastic gradient descent against a closed-form least-squares DMD and unsurprisingly lost,
with a gate that did not behave as a meter. Freezing the DMD operator and training only the
gated branch on its residual is what turned the gate into an instrument.
A training recipe that diverges. Rollout training from a cold network diverges (we observed
exactly 1.000 relative error, i.e. a collapsed prediction). It must be applied as fine-tuning on a
converged one-step model, with gradient clipping.
Seed sensitivity. Out-of-distribution rollout numbers vary substantially across seeds (±0.075
at one-step); single-seed out-of-distribution claims should not be trusted, which is why Section 9
reports error bars and Section 8 should be read through them.
Scope. All experiments are one-dimensional, at N = 64, with a single nonlinear term whose
form we ourselves planted. Recovery correlations of 1.0000 indicate a well-specified problem,
not a difficult one: they validate the instrument, they do not demonstrate discovery on data

operator, not the exact diffusion operator, which caps how far even the structured model
transfers in amplitude. Structure preservation is verified a posteriori (energy monitoring) rather
than imposed by construction; a symplectic or conservative parametrization is the natural next
hardening step.


12     Conclusion

Inverting the objective from classification to physical modelling makes the learned branch legible.
In that regime the failure of a fixed spectral basis is a heterogeneity meter; the residual of a
linear propagator is a nonlinearity meter; a gated learned branch fires with the same rectified
law as the classification gate; the term that branch must contain is the term sparse regression
names; and putting that named term into the model is what buys extrapolation — in horizon, in
amplitude, and under rollout training, where structure is multiplied rather than replaced. The
practical reading for a modeller is short: measure first whether your fixed front-end is failing
and in which way, prefer a structured learned family over a free one, and treat the objective
value itself as a mis-specification alarm.


13     Reproducibility

NumPy/SciPy/PyTorch with fixed seeds. Every experiment is a short deterministic script
in experiments/ that regenerates the figure of the same name — one per section, namely
phys wp1 pilot, phys wp1 close, phys wp2 dmd, depth test, phys wp3 sindy, phys wp4 rollout
and phys wp4b hardening — each accompanied by a RESULTS note stating its findings and its
limits. Sparse regression is implemented directly (sequential thresholded least squares) with no
external dependency. Trajectory generation for the Burgers sweep is cached in a regenerable
archive; the scripts recompute it deterministically when the cache is absent.
